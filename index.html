Honors American Literature • Washington Irving • Part 1

Unpacking Irving's Allusions

An allusion references a person, text, figure, or tradition outside the story. Open each card to learn the background. Then return to Irving's words to decide what the reference contributes. The webpage supplies context, not the interpretation.

1. “Spare the Rod, Spoil the Child” (p. 3)

Background

The traditional proverb expresses the idea that adults must discipline children or risk spoiling them. It reflects older views of parental and school authority, including corporal punishment. Its sentiment has parallels in the biblical Book of Proverbs, though this familiar wording is not a direct biblical quotation.

Return to Irving

Look at how Ichabod disciplines his pupils, how the narrator describes his sense of duty, and how Ichabod justifies his behavior. Notice any gap between the narrator's language and the actions described.

AnalyzeWhat belief about teaching does the saying express? How do Ichabod's actions complicate it?
2. Cotton Mather (p. 5)

Background

Cotton Mather (1663–1728) was an influential New England Puritan minister and author who wrote about religion, history, providence, witchcraft, and supernatural events. His Wonders of the Invisible World (1693) is associated with the Salem witchcraft trials. Irving invokes Mather's reputation as a writer of frightening supernatural accounts.

Return to Irving

Ichabod teaches school and reads books, yet strongly believes stories of witchcraft. Notice what happens to his imagination after reading and listening to ghost stories.

AnalyzeWhat did Mather write about? Why might Ichabod's belief surprise us?
3. Knights-Errant (pp. 6–7)

Background

Knights-errant are wandering heroes of medieval romance who undertake dangerous quests, battle giants and monsters, demonstrate courage and chivalry, and sometimes rescue or try to win the affection of a lady. Later literature, including Don Quixote, parodies such adventures.

Return to Irving

Compare the heroic stakes of a traditional knight's adventure with Ichabod's pursuit of Katrina, his motives, and the obstacles he faces. Watch for Irving's grand language.

AnalyzeWhat makes a knight heroic? How is Ichabod like—or unlike—one?
4. Hercules and Achilles (pp. 7–8)

Background: Hercules

Hercules (Greek: Heracles) is a mythological hero renowned for exceptional physical strength and feats including the Twelve Labors. Calling someone “Herculean” emphasizes their great strength.

Background: Achilles

Achilles, a Greek hero of Homer's Iliad, is celebrated for his fighting ability but is also known for pride and tremendous anger, particularly when his honor is challenged.

Return to Irving

Identify which traits Brom shares with these heroes. Compare the high stakes of legendary battles to the local romantic rivalry in Sleepy Hollow.

AnalyzeWhich heroic traits fit Brom? What is exaggerated? Is Irving flattering or mocking him?
Big question: What does the reference suggest? How does it fit—or fail to fit—the character? What might Irving want readers to notice?
